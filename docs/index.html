OverviewAbstract

Large language models (LLMs) struggle with sequential spatial planning, but aggregate success rates provide little insight into where failures occur. We present a multi-scale benchmark that separates local passability (Fine), junction topology (Meso), and goal orientation (Macro). We evaluate three LLMs on 1,050 mazes spanning seven sizes and compare four input formats. Coordinate lists are the most effective text encoding overall.

Isolated probes assess individual spatial judgments, while one-shot planning requires the model to aggregate many such judgments into a complete route. At 10×10, complete-route success falls to 0 to 6%, while isolated-probe accuracy remains approximately 30 to 75%. Success on individual judgments therefore does not ensure reliable route generation. First errors occur mainly at the Meso (59%) and Fine (39%) scales, with Macro errors accounting for about 1%.

On 90 OpenStreetMap routes in three cities, GPT-4o’s isolated junction-choice accuracy reaches 64.7%, compared with 13.3% complete-route success. Most of its failed routes included a nonexistent street connection, often a final jump onto the destination. These findings suggest that difficulties in coordinating Fine, Meso, and Macro judgments contribute to route-planning failures.

Key FindingsFour modules, four questions

Three modules mirror the route-planning pipeline — input acquisition, multi-scale representation, and hierarchical route planning — and a fourth tests transfer to real street networks.

RQ1 · INPUT ACQUISITION

Which input format best supports route planning?

Among the three text formats, structured Coordinate text is the strongest in aggregate (mean SR 34%, vs. Words 27% and Map 15%). Picture was evaluated for GPT-4o only, which solved no maze from it (0/150). The models usually emit grid-legal local moves even when complete routes fail.

RQ2 · MULTI-SCALE REPRESENTATION

Where do LLMs fail across spatial scales?

At 10×10, one-shot success is 0–6% across models, while isolated-probe accuracy is roughly 30–75%. First errors occur mainly at the Meso (59%) and Fine (39%) scales, with Macro about 1%. Success on individual judgments does not ensure a successful sequence.

RQ3 · HIERARCHICAL ROUTE PLANNING

Which control should be delegated?

Assigning corridor-walking and dead-end retreat to a deterministic algorithm and querying the model only at junctions lifts GPT-4o by 70–92 percentage points at 7×7 to 15×15. Delegation roughly doubles the navigable size, but performance still declines at larger sizes.

RQ4 · STREET-NETWORK TRANSFER

Does the gap carry over to real streets?

On 90 OpenStreetMap routes, GPT-4o identified neighbors at 99.4% and chose junction exits at 64.7%, yet only 13.3% of complete routes succeeded. Of the 78 failed routes, 66 were stopped at a connection that does not exist in the street graph.

The BenchmarkDesign at a glance

Mazes are standard two-dimensional grids generated by a mix of depth-first-search (long corridors) and randomized Prim (many branches), with fixed seeds for reproducibility. By construction they are trees: any two open cells are connected by a unique path and there are no cycles, so every junction choice is unambiguously right or wrong. Difficulty (easy / medium / hard) is controlled by the number of decision points on the solution path, dead-end density, and a confusion ratio. Every maze is pre-annotated with per-cell passable directions and cell types; along the solution path, annotations include the ideal heading and the goal-reaching branch at each junction.

Solving it

When the robot keeps Fine, Meso, and Macro reasoning aligned step after step, it follows the unique path from S to G. On small mazes the models manage this; the benchmark's question is what breaks as the maze grows.

The three clips below replay the dominant ways route planning falls apart — the same failure signatures our first-error analysis counts: Meso topological choices (59%) and Fine local execution (39%).

Wrong junction MESO

At a branch point the robot picks a decoy branch (green arrow marks the goal-reaching one) and ends in a dead end. Wrong junction branches are the Meso first errors, the largest share overall.

Wall hit FINE

The robot emits a move into a wall cell — a local-passability error. It misreads which neighbours are open.

Teleport FINE

The robot jumps to a non-adjacent cell, a teleport. Fine degradation is dominated by teleports, not wall hits.

Module 4 · RQ4Transfer to real street networks

Module 4 tests whether the gap between route planning and isolated judgments carries over from tree mazes to cyclic urban street graphs: OpenStreetMap pedestrian networks for Munich, Tokyo, and Toronto. All street-network results are for GPT-4o.

How complete routes fail. Of the 78 failed routes, 66 were stopped at a connection that does not exist in the street graph; in 46 of these 66, the illegal step was the final one and landed on the destination. Seventy of the 78 failed routes would pass endpoint checks (output format, origin, destination, and node identifiers), so these failures are detectable only by checking the route against the network.
Junction choice exceeded the branch-aware random baseline in each of the three junction-count strata, although its pooled accuracy remained below the bearing-greedy heuristic (64.7% versus 77.4%). Above-chance performance therefore does not establish an advantage over a simple spatial heuristic. The branch-count and cost-margin results are exploratory and describe the difficulty of isolated junction choice.

Each city × stratum cell holds 10 routes: 5 chosen by a fixed pseudo-random rule and 5 chosen, before any model was run, from a diagnostic stratum in which a bearing-greedy heuristic errs or fails to reach the goal. Exits within 5 m or 1% of the best route cost all count as correct, so a junction can have more than one acceptable answer.
