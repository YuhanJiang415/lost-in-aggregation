OverviewAbstract

Large language models (LLMs) struggle with sequential spatial planning, but aggregate success rates provide little insight into where failures occur. We present a multi-scale benchmark that separates local passability (Fine), junction topology (Meso), and goal orientation (Macro). We evaluate three LLMs on 1,050 mazes spanning seven sizes and compare four input formats. Coordinate lists are the most effective text encoding overall.

Isolated probes assess individual spatial judgments, while one-shot planning requires the model to aggregate many such judgments into a complete route. At 10×10, complete-route success falls to 0 to 6%, while isolated-probe accuracy remains approximately 30 to 75%. Success on individual judgments therefore does not ensure reliable route generation. First errors occur mainly at the Meso (59%) and Fine (39%) scales, with Macro errors accounting for about 1%.

On 90 OpenStreetMap routes in three cities, GPT-4o’s isolated junction-choice accuracy reaches 64.7%, compared with 13.3% complete-route success. Most of its failed routes included a nonexistent street connection, often a final jump onto the destination. These findings suggest that difficulties in coordinating Fine, Meso, and Macro judgments contribute to route-planning failures.

Key FindingsThree modules, three questions

The benchmark mirrors the route-planning pipeline: how space is read in, how it is represented across scales, and how control should be divided between an LLM and an algorithm.

RQ1 · INPUT ACQUISITION

Coordinates are the best text encoding

Among the text formats, structured coordinate text is the strongest in aggregate (mean SR 34%, vs. Words 27% and Map 15%); from a rendered image, GPT-4o solved no maze (0/150). Local moves stay legal even as whole-path success vanishes, so the collapse is one of global path assembly.

RQ2 · MULTI-SCALE REPRESENTATION

Lost in aggregation

At 10×10, complete-route success is 0–6% while isolated Fine / Meso / Macro probes stay at roughly 30–75%. First errors fall on Meso (59%) and Fine (39%), almost never Macro (1%). Success on individual judgments does not ensure a successful sequence.

RQ3 · HIERARCHICAL ROUTE PLANNING

Delegation helps, up to a size

Handing per-step execution to a deterministic walker and querying the LLM only at junctions lifts GPT-4o by 70–92 points at 7×7 to 15×15 — but success declines by 20×20 and is near zero by 30×30.

The BenchmarkDesign at a glance

Mazes are 2-D grids generated by a mix of depth-first-search (long corridors) and randomized Prim (many branches), with fixed seeds for reproducibility. By construction they are trees: any two cells are connected by a unique path, so every junction choice is unambiguously right or wrong. Every maze ships with per-cell passable directions, cell types, the shortest path, and the goal-reaching branch at each junction.

Solving it

When the robot keeps Fine, Meso, and Macro reasoning aligned step after step, it follows the unique path from S to G. On small mazes the models manage this; the benchmark's question is what breaks as the maze grows.

The three clips below replay the dominant ways route planning falls apart — the same failure signatures our first-error analysis counts: Meso junction choices (59%) and Fine local execution (39%).

Wrong junction MESO

At a branch point the robot picks a decoy branch (green arrow marks the goal-reaching one) and dies in a dead-end. The single most common first error.

Wall hit FINE

The robot emits a move into a wall cell — a local-passability error. It misreads which neighbours are open.

Teleport FINE

External ValidationBeyond mazes: real street networks

Does the gap between isolated judgments and whole-route success survive outside fully observable tree mazes? We rebuilt the same three probes on OpenStreetMap pedestrian networks in Munich, Tokyo, and Toronto, where graphs have cycles and a junction can have more than one near-optimal exit.

Two cautions on reading these numbers. Junction choice beats the branch-aware chance level in every scale, but it stays below a simple bearing-greedy heuristic (64.7% vs. 77.4%), so above-chance accuracy is not evidence of an advantage over a basic spatial rule. And the branch-count and cost-margin associations are exploratory: they describe which isolated junctions are hard, not why complete routes fail.
